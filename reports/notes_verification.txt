# Economic Theory approach 

I would make the basic object a choice among production technologies for a stream of cases, where each technology differs in fixed cost, execution cost, error risk, and how effectively errors can be detected.

Let \(j \in \{H,P,S,C\}\) denote human, AI prompt, AI skill, and conventional code/program.

For technology \(j\), define:

$$
F_j = \text{fixed cost of creating the capability}
$$

$$
c_j = \text{cost of producing one case}
$$

$$
p_j = \Pr(\text{output is wrong before verification})
$$

and let

$$
L = \text{loss if an incorrect output is accepted}.
$$

The interesting part is verification.

Suppose after producing an answer you observe a verification signal \(s\). Let

$$
\pi_j(s)=\Pr(\text{wrong}\mid s,j).
$$

You don't need to assume Gaussian signals. The signal could be a unit test, another model's score, compiler output, an evaluator, a confidence statistic, or human inspection.

Let \(v\) denote the informativeness or precision of this signal. Formally, higher \(v\) means that the signal is more informative in the Blackwell sense about whether the output is correct.

Suppose that after seeing the signal you have two choices:

* accept the result, incurring expected loss \(L\pi_j(s)\)
* escalate, redo, or repair it at cost \(a_j\)

assuming for now that escalation removes the error.

Then the optimal decision after seeing \(s\) is

$$
\text{escalate iff } L\pi_j(s)>a_j.
$$

So the expected residual verification/error cost is

$$
B_j(v,L)
=
E_s\left[
\min\{L\pi_j(s;v),a_j\}
\right].
$$

This is a useful object. It combines three things you care about:

$$
\boxed{\text{error probability} \times \text{error cost} \times \text{ability to detect errors}}
$$

More informative verification signals reduce \(B_j\). A perfectly informative verifier lets you repair only actual mistakes. A completely uninformative verifier leaves you choosing between accepting the prior error probability \(p_jL\) and checking everything at cost \(a_j\):

$$
B_j(0,L)=\min\{p_jL,a_j\}.
$$

With perfect verification,

$$
B_j(\infty,L)=p_j a_j
$$

if detected errors are repaired at cost \(a_j\).

Thus expected per-case cost is

$$
m_j(v,L)
=
c_j+k_j(v)+B_j(v,L),
$$

where \(k_j(v)\) is the direct cost of running the verifier.

Now introduce your frequency dimension.

Suppose cases arrive according to a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\), and future costs are discounted continuously at rate \(r\). The present value of choosing technology \(j\) forever is

$$
\boxed{
C_j
=
F_j+
\frac{\lambda}{r}
m_j(v,L)
}
$$

or

$$
C_j
=
F_j+
\frac{\lambda}{r}
\left[
c_j+k_j(v)+B_j(v,L)
\right].
$$

This gives you almost exactly the conceptual diagram you described.

If instead you want a standard discrete discount factor \(\delta\), with \(\lambda\) expected cases per period:

$$
\boxed{
C_j
=
F_j+
\frac{\lambda}{1-\delta}m_j(v,L)
}
$$

assuming a stationary infinite horizon.

The key comparison between two technologies \(A\) and \(B\) is particularly simple. Suppose \(B\) has a larger fixed cost but lower expected marginal cost:

$$
F_B>F_A,\qquad m_B<m_A.
$$

Then \(B\) becomes worthwhile once frequency exceeds

$$
\boxed{
\lambda^*_{A,B}
=
r
\frac{F_B-F_A}
{m_A-m_B}
}
$$

or, with discrete discounting,

$$
\boxed{
\lambda^*_{A,B}
=
(1-\delta)
\frac{F_B-F_A}
{m_A-m_B}.
}
$$

This is the economic logic behind prompt \(\rightarrow\) skill \(\rightarrow\) program.

You might parameterize them approximately as:

| Technology | Fixed cost \(F\) | Per-case cost \(c\) | Machine verification benefit |
| ---------- | ---------------: | ------------------: | ---------------------------- |
| Human      |         very low |           very high | relatively small             |
| Prompt     |            ~zero |         medium-high | medium                       |
| Skill      |           medium |          medium-low | medium-high                  |
| Program    |             high |               ~zero | very high                    |

Then increasing \(\lambda\) pushes you horizontally from human/prompt toward skill/program because fixed costs are spread across more cases.

Increasing verification precision \(v\) disproportionately favors machine execution because automated output can cheaply enter a generate-test-repair loop.

Increasing \(L\), the cost of error, has a slightly subtler effect.

If programs have both low error rates and highly informative verification, increasing \(L\) expands the program region dramatically. A $100,000 software investment can be worthwhile even for fairly modest volumes when every undetected mistake costs $1 million.

But if the task is poorly machine-verifiable, increasing \(L\) can instead push production toward human judgment or human review.

So your original two-dimensional figure,

$$
(\lambda,\;v)
$$

really wants a third dimension:

$$
\boxed{L=\text{cost of an undetected error}.}
$$

You can think of \(L\) as shifting the boundaries in the frequency-verifiability plane.

There is also an important distinction I would add between **machine verifiability** and **human verifiability**.

Suppose there are two signals:

$$
s_M \sim \text{machine verifier}
$$

and

$$
s_H \sim \text{human judgment}.
$$

Some tasks have:

$$
v_M \gg v_H
$$

such as arithmetic or checking whether software passes tests.

Others have:

$$
v_H \gg v_M
$$

such as whether a logo looks good, whether an argument is persuasive, or whether a research idea is interesting.

This gives a more precise interpretation of why humans dominate the low-machine-verifiability corner. It isn't necessarily that humans make fewer initial errors. It can be that humans possess a much better **evaluation signal**.

You can therefore write

$$
B_j(v_M,v_H,L)
$$

where each production technology has access to some combination of the two signals.

For example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H &: s_H\\
P &: s_M \text{ plus optional } s_H\\
S &: s_M \text{ routinely, }s_H\text{ selectively}\\
C &: s_M\text{ heavily}
\end{aligned}
$$

That naturally produces human-in-the-loop systems as an interior solution rather than as a separate category.

There is another dimension I think is essential for your program-versus-skill distinction: **scope**.

Let

$$
q_j=\Pr(\text{a randomly arriving case lies within capability }j\text{'s scope}).
$$

Programs may have something like

$$
q_C=.70,\qquad p_C\mid\text{in scope}=.001
$$

while a skill might have

$$
q_S=.98,\qquad p_S=.05.
$$

So code is **narrow and precise**, while AI is **broad and noisy**.

If cases outside the program's scope are routed to a fallback technology \(f\), its effective marginal cost becomes

$$
m_C^{eff}
=
q_Cm_C+
(1-q_C)m_f.
$$

This makes your scope-reliability tradeoff explicit.

You then have three major forces determining the technology:

$$
\boxed{
\text{frequency}
\times
\text{verification}
\times
\text{consequence of error}
}
$$

plus scope as a property of the technology/task match.

I would draw the basic taxonomy like this:

|                                  | Low frequency   | Medium frequency          | High frequency      |
| -------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------- |
| **Low machine verifiability**    | Human           | Human / AI + human review | Human-guided system |
| **Medium machine verifiability** | Prompt          | Skill                     | Skill / program     |
| **High machine verifiability**   | Prompt / script | Skill / program           | Program             |

Then \(L\) shifts the boundaries. High \(L\) pushes toward whatever technology has the lowest **residual undetected error**, rather than simply the lowest production cost.

One particularly important implication follows from frequency interacting with error cost. The value of reliability scales with the number of cases:

$$
PV(\text{error losses})
\approx
\frac{\lambda}{r}pL.
$$

So increasing frequency does two things simultaneously. It amortizes fixed development costs, and it magnifies the economic cost of even tiny systematic error rates.

That points to an extension I would definitely include in a richer model: **correlated versus independent errors**.

The simple model assumes each case has an independent probability \(p_j\) of error. But program errors are often systematic. One bug can incorrectly process 100,000 cases.

You could decompose errors into

$$
E_{it}=A_{it}+D_j
$$

where \(A_{it}\) is idiosyncratic case-level error and \(D_j\) is a latent defect in the capability.

Programs might have:

* very low \(A_{it}\)
* potentially consequential \(D_j\)

while humans might have:

* relatively high \(A_{it}\)
* less correlated error across cases.

Skills could sit between them.

That adds a nice economics point: **scale increases the return to automation, but also increases the cost of systematic mistakes**.

So if I were reducing this to the smallest useful model, I would use four primitives:

$$
\boxed{
(\lambda,\;v,\;L,\;q)
}
$$

where

* \(\lambda\): frequency of cases
* \(v\): informativeness of verification
* \(L\): consequence of an undetected error
* \(q\): regularity/scope, meaning how much of the case distribution can be covered by a reusable capability

and four technologies:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Human},\quad
\text{Prompt},\quad
\text{Skill},\quad
\text{Program}.
}
$$

The central choice is then

$$
j^*
=
\arg\min_j
\left\{
F_j+
\frac{\lambda}{r}
\left[
c_j+
k_j(v)+
B_j(v,L)
\right]
\right\},
$$

with scope/fallback costs added when \(q_j<1\).
